    05How the prediction works

    Expected surprise. The average of past EPS surprises, shrunk half way to zero, because companies that beat habitually see analysts adjust over time.

    Beat probability. The average of two estimates: the Laplace smoothed historical beat rate, (beats + 1) / (reports + 2), and the probability that a normally distributed surprise with the expected mean and historical dispersion is positive.

    Scenarios. The EPS surprise is treated as normally distributed around the expected surprise with the historical dispersion. Bear is a miss (surprise below zero), bull is a beat larger than the company's usual margin, base is everything in between. Each scenario's EPS is the average outcome inside its region.

    Price reaction. An event study measures the one day move after each past report (next session for reports after the close). Where there are at least four observations with a positive relationship, a regression of move on surprise maps each scenario to a price; otherwise the average absolute move is scaled by the size of the surprise.

    Scenario probabilities. Bear is one minus the beat probability. The beat probability is split between base and bull in the proportions the distribution implies.

    Twelve month view. Forward EPS (high, average, low estimate) times the forward price to earnings (P/E) ratio, re rated +10% in the bull and −15% in the bear case.

    Limits. Eight to sixteen past reports are a small sample. Reactions depend more on guidance and positioning than on the EPS number itself, and consensus data can lag. The model is a structured starting point for judgement, and its stated confidence is low by design. EPS = earnings per share.
